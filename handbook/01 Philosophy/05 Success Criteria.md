# Success Criteria

> Part of the **[Philosophy](README.md)**.

## Purpose

Success criteria define how to recognise whether the engineering philosophy is being applied effectively.

They provide a practical way to evaluate engineering behaviour, decisions and outcomes without reducing engineering quality to a single metric.

## Guidance

The Philosophy stage succeeds when it influences real decisions, not when it exists only as documentation.

Engineers should be able to explain why decisions were made, how trade-offs were considered and how the chosen approach supports long-term value.

### Problems Are Clearly Understood

Engineering work should be connected to a clear problem, objective or outcome.

Teams should be able to explain what problem is being solved, who it affects and why the work matters before committing to a solution.

### Decisions Are Intentional

Important decisions should be deliberate, explainable and based on the best available understanding.

Where trade-offs exist, they should be visible. Where assumptions exist, they should be acknowledged.

### Solutions Remain Understandable

Software should be understandable to engineers beyond the original authors.

Naming, structure, documentation and tests should help future maintainers understand the intent of the system.

### Quality Is Built In

Quality should be considered throughout discovery, design, implementation, testing, deployment and operations.

Reliability, maintainability, security and operability should not be postponed until the end of delivery.

### Complexity Is Justified

Complexity should have a clear reason.

Patterns, abstractions, tools and processes should solve real problems. If they do not improve the outcome, they should be simplified or removed.

### Learning Improves the System

Engineering teams should learn from feedback, incidents, reviews and delivery experience.

Success means the system, documentation and practices improve as understanding grows.

## Key Takeaways

- Engineering philosophy succeeds when it shapes real decisions.
- Work should be tied to clearly understood problems and outcomes.
- Important decisions should be intentional and explainable.
- Maintainability depends on systems remaining understandable.
- Quality should be designed into the lifecycle.
- Complexity should be justified by clear value.
- Learning should improve both the system and the team.
